# HardSync course catalog and content blueprint

## Product promise

Help new managers build practical skills through realistic, safe-to-try scenarios. Offer a brief optional read in the same flow so learners can prepare, practise, and try again.

**Audience:** first-time and experienced managers, engineering and product leaders, and emerging leaders.

The separate general-practice tab contains open-choice scenarios that are not part of a learning byte and the custom-scenario option. General and custom practice do not show quick-byte hints.

## Course-end practice brief

Each quick read leads directly to a matching practice scenario. Give the learner a short context, counterpart relationship, stakes, and one skill to try. Practice can use text, audio, or video:

- **Text:** low-pressure branching exchange; pause to choose or write a response.
- **Audio:** practice pacing, pauses, listening, and repair in a spoken conversation.
- **Video:** practice visual presence and turn-taking with a counterpart.

All three formats use the same course skill and a consistent rubric: clarity, listening, specificity, boundary or goal alignment, and agreed next step. Feedback should cite observable words or moments, show one alternative, and offer a retry. Do not grade personality, accent, eye contact, or emotional display as a proxy for leadership ability.

**Optional preparation:** show a short scenario choice and one prompt: â€œWhat will you try in a real conversation this week?â€ Then let the learner begin the live rehearsal immediately.

## Evidence and editorial guardrails

- Research supports practice on skills that are observable and relevant to the learnerâ€™s real task. Keep lessons short, concrete, and paired with retrieval/reflection and repeated application; treat the exact lesson duration and sequence above as product design recommendations, not research-proven thresholds.
- Feedback can help or harm depending on how it is delivered. Keep it task-focused, specific, contextual, and actionable; invite the receiverâ€™s perspective rather than treating feedback as a one-way verdict.
- Psychological safety is not avoiding accountability. Teach respectful candor, clear expectations, and the ability to raise questions or mistakes without humiliation.
- Use simple, descriptive steps rather than branded framework names. Where a useful model informs a lesson, explain the idea in plain language and show it through an example.
- Scenarios should include more than one reasonable response. Do not present a single magic phrase as guaranteed to work across cultures, roles, or power differences.
- The courses teach communication practice, not HR, legal, mental-health, or crisis advice. For harassment, discrimination, safety threats, or formal performance processes, direct learners to their organizationâ€™s appropriate support and policy.
